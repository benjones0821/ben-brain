### Claude · 2026-08-22 18:20

> *Claude used visualize:read_me*

Here's a full identity system for Aurange — the mark reads as an **A** built from a straight upstroke and a curved sweep, which doubles as the *aura* (the arc) and the *range* (the reach). The gold ties to **Au**, aurum, the first two letters of the name.

> *Claude used visualize:show_widget*

**Why it works**

The name gives you three usable ideas at once, and the mark carries all three without illustrating any of them literally. The straight left stroke and the curved right stroke meeting at an apex make an unmistakable A, but the curve breaks the symmetry a normal A has — that asymmetry is the whole personality. Straight side reads as structure; curved side reads as reach, signal, orbit. The crossbar is a slight arc rather than a flat line, which keeps the geometry from feeling like a default typeface letter.

**The system**

- Colors: ink `#2C2C2A`, aurum gold `#EF9F27`, deep gold `#BA7517` for the `u` in the wordmark, warm off-white `#F1EFE8`. Two hues only — one neutral, one brand — which is what makes it survive on merch, invoices, and a favicon.
- Wordmark: lowercase, medium weight, generous letter-spacing. Lowercase reads as approachable infrastructure rather than legacy enterprise; the tinted `u` quietly plants the Au reference without needing anyone to get the joke.
- Clear space: one stroke-width of the mark on all sides. Minimum mark size 20px — the crossbar arc is the first thing to break below that.
- Lockups: horizontal (shown) for web headers and email signatures; stack the mark above the wordmark for square placements like conference backdrops or app store listings.

**Where to stress-test it next**

Try the mark at 16px in a browser tab, and try the reversed version on a photograph. Those two cases kill more logos than anything else. If the crossbar muddies at 16px, thicken it to match the legs and shorten it slightly — the silhouette matters more than the detail at that size.

I can also build a version with the sweep as a full open circle around the A (more "aura," less "range"), or a sharper geometric take with flat caps if you want the brand to feel more infrastructural and less human.

Since Aurange bundles three ideas — *Au* (gold), *aura*, and *range* — here are alternatives grouped by which thread they pull on. Names that keep the "Au" root can reuse the mark I built as-is.

**Keeps the gold / Au thread**

**Keeps the reach / range thread**

- **Farline** — spatial and slightly poetic; strong logo potential with a horizon mark.

**Abstract and invented, maximum ownability**

**Plain-word, confident**
- **Meridian** — heavily used already, but instantly credible; usually needs a qualifier.

Two practical notes: exact-match .com domains for pronounceable five-to-six letter names are almost all taken or priced in the thousands, so plan on a modifier like `getauren.com` or a .ai/.co, and run a USPTO search in your specific class before falling in love with one.

### Claude · 2026-08-23 19:10

Different name, different physics. **Volvera** comes from Latin *volvere* — to turn, to revolve — with *vera* (true) sitting inside it. So where Aurange was warm and radiating, this one should be cool and rotational: a mark with momentum rather than glow.

> *Claude used visualize:show_widget*

**The idea**

The left arm is a straight diagonal — that's the fixed reference, the *vera*, the thing that doesn't move. The right arm leaves the vertex and keeps going: up, over, and hooking back toward the center, which is the *volvere*. The stroke stops just short of closing the loop, and the dot sits in the gap it's rotating toward. Incompleteness is what creates the sense of motion; if the hook closed into a full circle, the mark would go static and read as a generic app icon.

The dot also does structural work. Without it the top-left is a dead void and the whole mark leans right. With it, the composition balances and you get an implied orbital relationship — a body and the path around it.

**System specs**

- Ink `#26215C`, orbit violet `#7F77DD`, deep violet `#534AB7` for wordmark accents, pale ground `#EEEDFE`. On dark, the orbit lightens to `#AFA9EC` so it doesn't muddy against the indigo field.
- The accented `v` in the wordmark is the second one — it's the one the eye lands on mid-word, and it echoes the mark's moving arm rather than its fixed one.
- Clear space: the diameter of the dot on all sides. Minimum 22px — slightly larger floor than Aurange, because the gap between hook and dot is the first thing to close up at small sizes.
- Stroke weight is uniform at 18 units. Resist the urge to taper the orbit arm; tapering makes it read as a swoosh, and swooshes date badly.

**Honest weaknesses**

The V-with-a-curved-arm territory is crowded — you'll want to check it against Vercel, Vimeo, and anything in the automotive space before committing. And the hook direction is a real decision: as drawn it rotates counterclockwise, which subtly reads as *rewinding* to Western eyes. Mirroring the mark so the orbit sweeps clockwise would feel more like progress, at the cost of a slightly less natural V.

I can render the mirrored version, a variant where the orbit closes fully into a ring with the V cut from negative space, or a flat-cap geometric take if you want it to feel more like infrastructure and less like motion design.

### Claude · 2026-08-23 19:15

Here's the yellow system on the existing mark.

> *Claude used visualize:show_widget*

Yellow is the hardest color in identity work, because it has almost no contrast against white — a pure yellow logo on a white page is nearly invisible. The fix is to pair it with a very dark warm neutral: ink `#412402`, orbit `#EF9F27`, wordmark accent `#BA7517`, ground `#FAEEDA`, and a lighter `#FAC775` for the orbit when it sits on the dark tile. The dark ink does the legibility work so the yellow can stay genuinely yellow.

Now, three alternate directions for the mark itself — same name, different personalities.

> *Claude used visualize:show_widget*

**Orbit ring** is the safest and the most institutional. The gap at the top keeps it from being a plain circle-in-a-box, and the enclosed form scales down better than anything else here — it holds at 16px where the others start to blur. Downside: enclosed roundel marks are everywhere, so it earns credibility at the cost of distinctiveness.

**Nested** trades rotation for convergence. Three layers reading inward suggests depth, resolution, things narrowing to a point — good if the product is about filtering signal out of noise. It's the most flexible for animation, since the layers can cascade. Weakness: at small sizes the gaps between chevrons fill in and it becomes a solid triangle.

**Blade** is the boldest. Solid mass instead of stroke, flat caps instead of round, and the two halves meeting at a shared flat base so the color split reads as one object rather than two. This is the one that would look best on a truck, a hard hat, or a piece of hardware. It's also the least warm — nothing about it is friendly, which is either exactly right or exactly wrong depending on who's buying.

If I had to pick for a software company, I'd take the original orbit mark for distinctiveness and keep **Orbit ring** as the app icon variant, since enclosed forms survive the small-size test better. Tell me the category and I'll narrow it and build out whichever direction you want at full lockup scale.
