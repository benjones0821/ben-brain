> The National Railroad Passenger Corporation, better known as Amtrak, began operations in 1971. The railroad has more than 20,000 employees and serves more than 500 destinations in the United States and Canada on more than 21,400 miles of track. Amtrak customers took 32.5 million trips in 2019, setting a record year-over-year increase of 800,000 passengers.1
> 
>  
> 
> Congress created Amtrak because private railroads were failing. By the 1940s, rail travel became less popular as Americans chose buses, planes, and cars to get around the country. Eventually, the U.S. government consolidated the majority of passenger rail service under Amtrak’s umbrella. The federal government is Amtrak’s majority stockholder and guarantees its financial support, but the company is operated as a for-profit organization rather than a government entity.
> 
>  
> 
> Though it was created to save an unprofitable railroad system, Amtrak itself has never earned a profit since its inception. For example, the company lost $194 million and $170 million in 2017 and 2018, respectively. Americans continue to choose other modes of transportation over Amtrak, and government subsidies are all that stand between the railroad and bankruptcy.2
> 
>  
> 
> Let’s take a closer look at what’s going on at America’s only high-speed rail provider.

> A LOSING MODEL
> 
>  
> 
> One of Amtrak’s biggest problems is its price. For example, a four-hour Amtrak train from New York City to Boston is more expensive than hopping on a one-hour flight. Amtrak charges these high fares on popular Northeastern routes because its other routes across the country are either unprofitable or operating at a loss.3 According to Virginia Mercury, ridership may be able to grow if Amtrak’s prices were reduced.4
> 
>  
> 
> Amtrak’s other challenge is America’s sheer size. It is the fourth largest country in the world with 3.8 million square miles of land. Compare this with Japan’s rail service, which has to cover an area smaller than the state of California. All of this rail needs maintenance and repair, which Amtrak can’t afford. For example, simply bringing the rail tracks in the 453-mile Northeast Corridor to a state of good repair will cost $42 billion.5
> 
>  
> 
> To make matters worse, Amtrak’s rails aren’t the only asset in dire need of fixing. Its passenger cars have expected lifespans of 25 years, yet the average car in its fleet is well over 30 years old. And Amtrak’s biggest investor, the federal government, which has spent more than $100 billion in taxpayer funds to keep the rail service operating, doesn’t have the appetite to make largescale investments in the railroad.6
> 
>  
> 
> LEADERSHIP HAS A NEW PLAN
> 
>  
> 
> Amtrak hired Richard Anderson as CEO in 2017 to chart a new strategy for the company. Anderson, formerly Delta Airlines’ CEO, is changing the railroad’s route system and the services it provides in the hopes of leading it to profitability for the first time in its 50-year history.
> 
>  
> 
> The first part of Anderson’s plan includes breaking up long-distance train routes and substituting bus service. For example, he wants to change the Chicago to Los Angeles route and replace 500 miles of the trip with bus service. Anderson believes these types of routes don’t meet the needs of today’s commuters. “If we’re going to deal with congestion, growing populations and the carbon footprint of automobiles, Amtrak is the best answer for intercity transportation in a 200- to 300-mile market,” he says.7 Reducing the number of unprofitable routes won’t only help the carrier’s bottom line, it will also reduce the costs associated with maintaining large swaths of railroad tracks.
> 
>  
> 
> The second part of Anderson’s plan includes a reduction in some of the company’s services. For example, Amtrak has decided to scrap its traditional dining car service. The reservation-based dining cars, staples of the U.S. railroad system, had shiny silverware and white linens. Fresh food, which could rival high-end restaurants, was provided by onboard kitchens. Now, Amtrak provides prepackaged meals and no longer has “white glove” service in most of its dining cars. The company estimates that this change will save around $2 million a year and attract a younger generation of new riders who are on the run, on their phones, and not looking to sit at a fancy, communal table. The change “is part of an evolution,” says Peter Wilander, Amtrak’s customer service chief.8
> 
>  
> 
> DERAILED PLANS?
> 
>  
> 
> Amtrak’s strategic plan is showing some early success. The company posted a $29.8 million loss in 2019, its best operating performance ever.9 And the company believes 2020 will be even better. “Our expectation is that in 2020, we will actually make money, we will have positive earnings for the first time in the company’s history,” says Amtrak Board Chairman Anthony Coscia.10
> 
>  
> 
> However, critics believe Anderson’s pursuit of profitability has caused Amtrak’s service to suffer. Jim Mathews, president and chief executive of the Rail Passengers Association, says train riders are lamenting the end of the dining car. “It is not just the food, it is the experience,” he tells The Washington Post. Skeptical lawmakers, responsible for subsidizing the company, echo Mathews’ concerns. “This is shortsighted and foolish,” says Tennessee Congressman Steve Cohen. Representative Cohen feels Anderson is doing to rail service what he did to air service. Amtrak’s decision is “like Delta Air Lines taking away amenities to passengers on their airplanes and making air traffic more like traveling on a bus,” he says.11
> 
>  
> 
> Lawmakers, many from less populated states, have also taken aim at Anderson’s plans to cut long-haul routes, which include stops in states like Kansas and New Mexico. “The idea that Amtrak would think about replacing passenger service with bus service for 400 miles and believe that we would still have a long-distance passenger train service is something I can’t get over,” said Kansas Senator Jerry Moran. Moran’s criticism doesn’t just put Amtrak’s congressional budget allocation in jeopardy. The government also has broad discretion to direct the railroad to take or not take certain actions. In this case, the Senate ordered Amtrak to run its Southwest long-haul route as originally planned, shattering Anderson’s strategy.12
> 
>  
> 
> Other lawmakers think the company’s entire model should be re-evaluated. Oregon Congressman Peter DeFazio believes Anderson’s profit-focused philosophy is inappropriate for a government-owned company like Amtrak. “I think part of the problem we’re dealing with is the original mandate from Congress, which said that [Amtrak] is supposed to be run as a for-profit corporation,” says DeFazio.13

I'll check the textbook definition of an operational plan, then match it against the options.

> *Claude used WebSearch: Kinicki Williams management strategic tactical operational planning definition first-line managers*

**Answer: developing a schedule for repurposing dining cars**

In this textbook (Kinicki & Williams, *Management*), planning happens at three levels. Strategic planning is done at the top level, tactical planning at the middle level, and operational planning at the frontline; strategic planning defines goals, tactical planning turns those goals into specific unit plans, and operational planning identifies processes. Operational plans are highly detailed and are made by lower or first-line management. The textbook also describes operational planning as deciding how to accomplish specific tasks within a year or less.

Here is how each option fits:

- **Developing a schedule for repurposing dining cars** is **operational**. A schedule is a detailed, short-term, task-level plan that a frontline manager would make to carry out the dining-car change.
- **Breaking up long-distance train routes** is **strategic or tactical**. It is part of Anderson's top-level plan to change the route system.
- **Reevaluating the company's mission and vision statements** is **strategic**. That is top management's job and sets long-term direction.
- **Becoming profitable in the next 12 months** is **strategic**. It is an overall organizational goal, even though it has a one-year timeframe. Long-term objectives such as overall profitability belong to strategic planning.
- **Reducing the company's services** is **strategic or tactical**. It is a broad initiative from senior management, not a specific procedure.

The one-year timeframe in "becoming profitable in the next 12 months" can be tempting, but it is a company-wide outcome goal. Only the dining-car schedule describes the detailed "how" that frontline managers handle.

**Answer: developing a schedule for repurposing dining cars**

I checked this again against the textbook's definition and the answer holds.

**The test for an operational plan.** In the Kinicki/Williams framing, operational planning is when first-line managers determine how to accomplish specific tasks with available resources in the upcoming year. Operational plans are executed and finalized by first-line managers for short-term targets of less than a year (usually monthly, weekly or daily). So the right answer has to be specific, task-level, short-term, and about how something gets done, not what the company wants to achieve.

**Each option against that test:**

| Option | Level | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Breaking up long-distance train routes | Strategic | This is the first part of the CEO's plan. It changes the company's whole route structure. |
| **Developing a schedule for repurposing dining cars** | **Operational** | A schedule is a detailed, short-term, task-level plan a frontline manager would make to carry out the dining-car decision. |
| Reevaluating the mission and vision statements | Strategic | This is top management deciding the organization's direction. |
| Becoming profitable in the next 12 months | Strategic goal | Strategic plans include overall profitability. It is a company-wide result, not a task. |
| Reducing the company's services | Strategic | This is the second part of the CEO's plan, a broad decision rather than a procedure. |

**Why the other options don't fit.** "Becoming profitable in the next 12 months" is the most tempting wrong answer because its timeframe matches operational plans. It still says *what* the company wants, not *how* a frontline unit does a specific task. "Reducing services" and "breaking up routes" are the two parts of the CEO's top-level plan. The dining-car schedule is the frontline step that carries out one of those decisions, which is the plan hierarchy the textbook describes: operational plans are necessary to attain tactical plans and tactical plans lead to the achievement of strategic plans.

> Though it was created to save an unprofitable railroad system, Amtrak itself has never earned a profit since its inception. For example, the company lost $194 million and $170 million in 2017 and 2018, respectively. Americans continue to choose other modes of transportation over Amtrak, and government subsidies are all that stand between the railroad and bankruptcy.2
>  
> Let’s take a closer look at what’s going on at America’s only high-speed rail provider.
>  
> A LOSING MODEL
>  
> One of Amtrak’s biggest problems is its price. For example, a four-hour Amtrak train from New York City to Boston is more expensive than hopping on a one-hour flight. Amtrak charges these high fares on popular Northeastern routes because its other routes across the country are either unprofitable or operating at a loss.3 According to Virginia Mercury, ridership may be able to grow if Amtrak’s prices were reduced.4
>  
> Amtrak’s other challenge is America’s sheer size. It is the fourth largest country in the world with 3.8 million square miles of land. Compare this with Japan’s rail service, which has to cover an area smaller than the state of California. All of this rail needs maintenance and repair, which Amtrak can’t afford. For example, simply bringing the rail tracks in the 453-mile Northeast Corridor to a state of good repair will cost $42 billion.5
>  
> To make matters worse, Amtrak’s rails aren’t the only asset in dire need of fixing. Its passenger cars have expected lifespans of 25 years, yet the average car in its fleet is well over 30 years old. And Amtrak’s biggest investor, the federal government, which has spent more than $100 billion in taxpayer funds to keep the rail service operating, doesn’t have the appetite to make largescale investments in the railroad.6
>  
> LEADERSHIP HAS A NEW PLAN
>  
> Amtrak hired Richard Anderson as CEO in 2017 to chart a new strategy for the company. Anderson, formerly Delta Airlines’ CEO, is changing the railroad’s route system and the services it provides in the hopes of leading it to profitability for the first time in its 50-year history.
>  
> The first part of Anderson’s plan includes breaking up long-distance train routes and substituting bus service. For example, he wants to change the Chicago to Los Angeles route and replace 500 miles of the trip with bus service. Anderson believes these types of routes don’t meet the needs of today’s commuters. “If we’re going to deal with congestion, growing populations and the carbon footprint of automobiles, Amtrak is the best answer for intercity transportation in a 200- to 300-mile market,” he says.7 Reducing the number of unprofitable routes won’t only help the carrier’s bottom line, it will also reduce the costs associated with maintaining large swaths of railroad tracks.
>  
> The second part of Anderson’s plan includes a reduction in some of the company’s services. For example, Amtrak has decided to scrap its traditional dining car service. The reservation-based dining cars, staples of the U.S. railroad system, had shiny silverware and white linens. Fresh food, which could rival high-end restaurants, was provided by onboard kitchens. Now, Amtrak provides prepackaged meals and no longer has “white glove” service in most of its dining cars. The company estimates that this change will save around $2 million a year and attract a younger generation of new riders who are on the run, on their phones, and not looking to sit at a fancy, communal table. The change “is part of an evolution,” says Peter Wilander, Amtrak’s customer service chief.8
>  
> DERAILED PLANS?
>  
> Amtrak’s strategic plan is showing some early success. The company posted a $29.8 million loss in 2019, its best operating performance ever.9 And the company believes 2020 will be even better. “Our expectation is that in 2020, we will actually make money, we will have positive earnings for the first time in the company’s history,” says Amtrak Board Chairman Anthony Coscia.10
>  
> However, critics believe Anderson’s pursuit of profitability has caused Amtrak’s service to suffer. Jim Mathews, president and chief executive of the Rail Passengers Association, says train riders are lamenting the end of the dining car. “It is not just the food, it is the experience,” he tells The Washington Post. Skeptical lawmakers, responsible for subsidizing the company, echo Mathews’ concerns. “This is shortsighted and foolish,” says Tennessee Congressman Steve Cohen. Representative Cohen feels Anderson is doing to rail service what he did to air service. Amtrak’s decision is “like Delta Air Lines taking away amenities to passengers on their airplanes and making air traffic more like traveling on a bus,” he says.11
>  
> Lawmakers, many from less populated states, have also taken aim at Anderson’s plans to cut long-haul routes, which include stops in states like Kansas and New Mexico. “The idea that Amtrak would think about replacing passenger service with bus service for 400 miles and believe that we would still have a long-distance passenger train service is something I can’t get over,” said Kansas Senator Jerry Moran. Moran’s criticism doesn’t just put Amtrak’s congressional budget allocation in jeopardy. The government also has broad discretion to direct the railroad to take or not take certain actions. In this case, the Senate ordered Amtrak to run its Southwest long-haul route as originally planned, shattering Anderson’s strategy.12
>  
> Other lawmakers think the company’s entire model should be re-evaluated. Oregon Congressman Peter DeFazio believes Anderson’s profit-focused philosophy is inappropriate for a government-owned company like Amtrak. “I think part of the problem we’re dealing with is the original mandate from Congress, which said that [Amtrak] is supposed to be run as a for-profit corporation,” says DeFazio.13
>  
> Anderson’s plans seem to be stalling as the company is on the verge of profitability.

### Claude · 2026-10-13 18:25

These line up one-to-one with the standard VRIO outcomes:

| Item | Match |
|---|---|
| Does not allow it to exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat | **2 – Competitive disadvantage** |
| No worse than the competition, but no better either | **1 – Equally competitive** |
| Not particularly costly for other firms to imitate | **4 – Temporary competitive advantage** |
| Valuable, rare, and costly to imitate | **3 – Sustained competitive advantage** |

The VRIO questions build on each other:

- **Not valuable:** the resource doesn't help exploit opportunities or neutralize threats, so the firm is at a **competitive disadvantage**.
- **Valuable but not rare:** competitors have it too, so the firm is at **parity**, or equally competitive.
- **Valuable and rare but easy to imitate:** the firm has an edge only until rivals copy it, which is a **temporary advantage**.
- **Valuable, rare, costly to imitate, and the firm is organized to use it:** this gives a **sustained advantage**. The last item leaves out "organized," but it is the only one that passes the first three tests, so it gets the sustained answer.
