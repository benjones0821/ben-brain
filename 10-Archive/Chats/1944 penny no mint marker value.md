1944 penny no mint marker how much

---

### Claude · 2026-10-03 18:42

A 1944 wheat penny with no mint mark (struck in Philadelphia) is very common, about 1.4 billion were made.

- **Circulated (worn):** about 3 to 10 cents
- **Lightly worn, nice detail:** 15 to 50 cents
- **Uncirculated, brown/red-brown:** $1 to $5
- **Uncirculated, full red, graded:** $10 to $30+, with top grades (MS67 red) reaching several hundred dollars

**The exception worth checking:** a 1944 penny struck on a leftover steel planchet. These are rare and sell for tens of thousands of dollars or more. Test with a magnet: a steel cent sticks and looks silver-gray; a normal copper one doesn't stick and weighs about 3.1 g (steel is about 2.7 g). Be aware that many "steel" 1944s are fakes or copper cents that were plated, so a real candidate needs authentication by PCGS or NGC.

Prices shift over time, so for a coin in great shape, check recent sold listings on eBay or the PCGS price guide.

Is 408k a possible price

### Claude · 2026-10-03 18:42

Yes, but only for the steel error, and that exact figure belongs to a different coin than yours. A 1944-S steel cent (San Francisco mint, with an "S" under the date) graded MS66 sold for $408,000 at Heritage Auctions in 2021. Only two 1944-S steel cents are known.

For a no-mint-mark (Philadelphia) 1944 steel cent, about 25 to 30 are known, and they've typically sold for roughly $30,000 to $180,000 depending on condition.

A normal copper 1944 penny is never worth anything close to that, regardless of what online listings ask. Those $408k headlines often get attached to ordinary coins in clickbait articles and overpriced eBay listings.

So the magnet test is the whole question: if it doesn't stick, it's a common coin worth cents to a few dollars. If it does stick, it's worth getting authenticated by PCGS or NGC.

What grade would it be to be crazy

---

### Claude · 2026-10-03 18:44

For a normal copper 1944 no-mint-mark penny, it would need to be essentially perfect: **MS67+ Red or MS68 Red**.

- **MS65 to MS66 Red:** $10 to $40
- **MS67 Red:** roughly $100 to $300
- **MS67+ Red:** around $1,000 to a few thousand
- **MS68 Red:** the top of the scale for this coin, only a handful exist, and they've sold in the range of several thousand to low five figures

Those figures are approximate, so check the PCGS price guide or recent auction results for current numbers.

"Red" means the coin still has its full original bright copper-orange color, like the day it was minted. A coin at these grades has never been spent, has no visible scratches or spots even under magnification, and has usually sat in a roll or collection since 1944. Anything found in pocket change, a jar, or an old drawer loose with other coins is almost certainly not in this range, and cleaning a coin to make it shiny destroys its value.

If it's the steel version, grade barely matters: even a worn one is worth tens of thousands.

### Claude · 2026-10-03 18:46

A filled-in "P" is a minor, common error (grease or a small chip clogged the die), and it adds little: typically **$1 to $5** over the coin's normal value, sometimes nothing.

The more interesting part is the date. 1983-P quarters are worth more than most modern quarters in high grades, because the Mint made no official mint sets that year and few people saved nice ones.

- **Circulated (worn, from change):** 25 cents, maybe $1 to $3 with the filled P to an error collector
- **Uncirculated, average (MS63 to MS64):** about $10 to $30
- **MS65:** about $40 to $70
- **MS66:** around $100 to $200
- **MS67:** $1,000 or more

Those are approximate, so check recent eBay sold listings or the PCGS price guide for current numbers.

So the value comes down to wear, not the mint mark. If it has been in circulation and shows any flattening on Washington's hair or the eagle's breast feathers, it's a keeper for fun but not a money coin. If it looks brand new with full luster and no wear, it could be worth sending for grading.
