### Claude · 2026-09-22 15:08

Here's a discovery script built around your five levers. I kept only the questions that either reveal a leak or help you put a dollar figure on it. Under each question is what to listen for.

## Opener (earn the next 2 minutes)
1. **"What does a good month look like for you right now, and how far off that are you?"** Their own gap is your first number.
2. **"If you could fix one thing about how customers find you or get booked, what would it be?"** They often name the leak for you.

## Volume: are enough people coming in?
3. **"Where do most of your new customers come from today?"** If the only answer is word of mouth or Google, nothing is working to bring them in.
4. **"Roughly how many new inquiries do you get in a normal week?"** This is your baseline.
5. **"Do you ask customers for a review after a job? How?"** If it's "we tell them to" or "sometimes," pitch review capture.
6. **"Do you have a list of past customers or people who got a quote but didn't buy? Do you ever contact them?"** A list that sits unused means pitch email and a newsletter.

## Velocity: how fast do leads get answered?
7. **"When someone calls after 5pm or on a weekend, what happens?"** Voicemail or nothing means pitch speed to lead.
8. **"When a web form or email comes in, who sees it and how fast do they reply?"** "When I get to it" is your biggest leak for most small shops.
9. **"How many calls do you think you miss in a week?"** Most owners guess low. That's fine, because it gives you the sizing input.
10. **"How do people book with you? Phone, online, back-and-forth texts?"** Back-and-forth booking means pitch calendar handoff.
11. **"Do you get no-shows? About how many a week?"** This is easy money to recover with reminders.

## Conversion: do leads turn into jobs?
12. **"Out of 10 people who get a quote or consult, how many say yes?"** Under about 4 means there's a conversion problem.
13. **"What happens to the ones who don't say yes right away? Does anyone follow up?"** This is usually the second-biggest leak.
14. **"Where do you keep track of leads and customers?"** A notebook, their head or a spreadsheet means pitch a CRM.
15. **"When was your website last updated? Do people actually contact you through it?"** Nobody using the site means pitch website fixes.
16. **"If you're not on the phone, who handles sales calls? Do they say roughly the same thing every time?"** If each person handles it differently, pitch a sales script and coaching.

## Value: are they selling to the right people?
17. **"Who's your best type of customer, and who's the kind you wish you didn't take?"**
18. **"Which services make you the most money? Are you getting enough of those?"** A mismatch means pitch ICP work and repositioning.

## Money & Time Saved: where the owner's week goes
19. **"How many hours a week do you spend on admin like emails, scheduling and answering the same questions?"**
20. **"If your best employee quit tomorrow, what knowledge would walk out with them?"** This sets up the second-brain pitch.
21. *(Healthcare, legal or financial only)* **"How do you handle compliance when staff use new tools or AI?"** This sets up the governance kit.

## Sizing and close (always ask these)
22. **"What's an average job or customer worth to you, both first visit and over time?"** Multiply this by missed leads and lost quotes to get the size of the leak.
23. **"Have you tried to fix any of this before? What happened?"** This surfaces objections early.
24. **"If we could show you exactly where you're losing money in 20 minutes, free, would that be worth it?"** This books the Funnel Read.

**The quick math to say out loud:** missed leads per week × close rate × average job value × 52 = what the leak costs them per year. Owners respond to their own numbers far more than yours.

If you only get 5 minutes on a cold call, ask #1, #7, #9, #13 and #22. Those five are usually enough to name and size one leak.

### Claude · 2026-09-22 15:15

Stubborn owners usually aren't saying no to you. They're protecting something: their time, their pride in how they run things, or bad memories of past "marketing guys." The goal is to lower their guard, not to win the argument.

## The mindset
- **Don't push harder, agree more.** Pushing makes them dig in. Agreeing takes away what they're resisting.
- **Let their numbers argue for you.** An owner will dismiss your claims but not their own answers. Keep asking questions instead of making statements.
- **Shrink the ask.** Nobody is stubborn about 20 free minutes if it feels truly low-stakes.
- **Use your real advantage.** Your site says that if the fix isn't worth building, you'll tell them so and they keep the findings. Most vendors can't say that. Say it out loud.

## Common pushback and what to say

**"We're fine, business is good."**
"That's great, and honestly most of the owners we talk to are doing well. I'm not assuming anything's broken. Can I ask one thing: when someone calls after 5, what happens to that call?" Then stop talking. Good businesses still leak; they just don't feel it.

**"We get all our business from word of mouth."**
"That's the best kind of business. Out of curiosity, how many reviews have you asked for this month?" A word-of-mouth business is exactly who review capture is built for, so frame it as scaling what already works rather than replacing it.

**"I've been burned by marketing companies before."**
"What happened?" Then really listen. Afterwards: "That's exactly why we don't sell anything until we've looked at your numbers. If there's nothing worth fixing, we'll tell you." Their bad experience becomes your contrast.

**"I don't have time."**
"Totally fair. That's actually the thing we fix most often. I only need 20 minutes, and you'll know where you're losing money either way. Is a morning or afternoon better next week?" Offer two specific times, not an open question.

**"Just send me some info."**
This is usually a polite no. Try: "Happy to. So I don't send you a generic brochure, what's the one thing you'd most want to fix?" If they answer, you're back in a conversation. If not, send something short and specific to their business, not a deck.

**"AI? No thanks."**
Don't defend AI. Talk about the outcome: "Forget the tech. You'd just have every call answered and every quote followed up. How it works behind the scenes is our problem." The word AI scares a lot of mom-and-pop owners, so lead with results.

**"How much does it cost?" (asked early, to shut you down)**
"I honestly can't tell you yet, because we only quote after we find what's actually broken. The diagnosis is free. If the fix costs more than the leak, we'll tell you not to do it."

## Tactics that work on stubborn people
- **Give them something for free right then.** Look them up before you call, then say something like: "I noticed you close at 4 on weekdays. Most of your competitors answer until 6." Handing over one useful insight earns respect fast.
- **Let them say no to something small.** "Is it crazy to think you might be missing a few calls a week?" Stubborn people love saying "no, not crazy." That's a yes in disguise.
- **Use social proof from their own world.** "A plumber in town told us the same thing, then realized he was missing 8 calls a week." Only say this once you have real cases to point to.
- **Take the pressure off at the end.** "It sounds like the timing isn't right, and that's okay. Mind if I check back in January?" A graceful exit often gets a callback, while a pushy one gets blocked.

## When to walk away
If after two genuine attempts they still won't engage, move on politely, note it and follow up in 60 to 90 days. Your time is better spent on the owner who already feels the pain. With a list like yours, there are plenty of those. A stubborn "no" today often becomes a "yes" after their busy season, when they finally notice the missed calls.
