## The 14 Points (paraphrased)

1. **Constancy of purpose** — commit to long-term improvement of product/service rather than short-term profit
2. **Adopt the new philosophy** — management must accept responsibility and lead the change; defects and delays are no longer acceptable
3. **Cease dependence on mass inspection** — build quality in instead of inspecting defects out at the end
4. **Stop awarding business on price tag alone** — move to long-term, single-source supplier relationships built on trust
5. **Improve constantly and forever** the system of production and service
6. **Institute training on the job**
7. **Institute leadership** — supervisors should coach and remove obstacles, not police
8. **Drive out fear** so people can raise problems and work effectively
9. **Break down barriers between departments** — engineering, sales, production, research work as one team
10. **Eliminate slogans, exhortations, and numerical targets** aimed at the workforce
11. **Eliminate numerical quotas and management by objective/numbers** on the shop floor and in management
12. **Remove barriers to pride of workmanship** — abolish annual merit ratings and management by objective
13. **Institute a vigorous program of education and self-improvement**
14. **Put everybody to work on the transformation** — it's everyone's job

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** [Instructor Name]
**FROM:** [Your Name]
**DATE:** August 11, 2026
**SUBJECT:** W. Edwards Deming's 14 Points — Selected Principles, Financial Impact, Corporate Adoption, and International Influence

---

**PURPOSE**

This memorandum examines six of W. Edwards Deming's 14 Points for Management, explains the operational benefit each provides to an adopting company, and assesses the likely effect of each on net income. It then reviews five companies that adopted Deming's system and reports whether profits rose in the two years following adoption. Finally, it describes Deming's influence abroad.

---

**PART I: SIX SELECTED POINTS AND THEIR BENEFITS**

**Point 3 — Cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality.**

Deming argued that mass inspection at the end of a production line is a failure of process design, not a quality program. Inspection does not create quality; it only sorts good units from bad after the money has already been spent making both. His alternative was to build quality into the process through statistical control, so that defects are prevented rather than detected.

*Benefit:* The company eliminates a non-value-adding activity, reduces scrap and rework, and stops shipping the defects that inspection inevitably misses. Warranty claims and returns decline.

*Effect on net income:* Positive, and among the most direct of the six. Inspection labor, rework labor, scrap material, and warranty reserves are all expenses that flow through cost of goods sold. Removing them raises gross margin with no increase in sales volume. There is a short-term offset — training and measurement systems must be paid for first — so net income may be flat or slightly depressed in year one before improving.

**Point 4 — End the practice of awarding business on price tag alone.**

Deming urged companies to abandon competitive bidding based on unit price and instead move toward long-term, single-source relationships built on trust and demonstrated quality. Purchasing on price alone, he argued, ignores the total cost of the transaction.

*Benefit:* A cheap component that arrives with variable dimensions creates downstream problems far more expensive than the savings: line stoppages, rework, engineering changes, and field failures. A stable supplier relationship reduces incoming variation, permits joint process improvement, and reduces the administrative cost of constant re-bidding.

*Effect on net income:* Mixed in the short term, positive over time. Unit purchase prices will very likely rise, increasing raw material cost. However, total cost falls as incoming defect rates decline, inventory buffers shrink, and production interruptions become rarer. Net income typically dips modestly in the transition year and improves thereafter. This point is the hardest of the six to defend to a purchasing department evaluated on price variance.

**Point 5 — Improve constantly and forever the system of production and service.**

Improvement is not a project with an end date but a permanent management obligation. Deming's Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle institutionalizes ongoing reduction of variation in every process, not just manufacturing.

*Benefit:* Costs decline continuously as variation is removed, productivity rises without capital investment, and quality improvements compound rather than plateau. Because improvement is permanent, competitors pursuing one-time fixes eventually fall behind.

*Effect on net income:* Strongly positive over a multi-year horizon and the single largest contributor of the six. Continuous reduction in scrap, cycle time, and rework directly lowers cost of goods sold, while improved reliability supports pricing power and repeat sales. The effect is small in any single quarter and substantial when compounded across years — which is precisely why Deming warned that the benefits cannot be evaluated on a quarterly basis.

**Point 6 — Institute training on the job.**

Deming observed that most workers are trained by other workers who were themselves trained inadequately, so error is transmitted rather than corrected. He called for formal, standardized training in the job itself and in the statistical methods used to control it.

*Benefit:* Workers understand what constitutes acceptable output and can distinguish normal process variation from a genuine special cause. Errors, startup scrap, and equipment damage fall. Training also reduces turnover cost, since employees who can succeed at their jobs are less likely to leave.

*Effect on net income:* Negative in the implementation period, positive thereafter. Training is an immediate expense — instructor cost, materials, and lost production time — recognized in the period incurred, while the resulting savings accrue over subsequent years. This timing mismatch means net income is reduced in year one. Companies unwilling to absorb that near-term charge generally abandon the system before the return arrives.

**Point 8 — Drive out fear.**

Deming considered fear the most expensive item on any income statement, though it appears on no line of it. Where employees fear blame, punishment, or job loss, they conceal defects, understate problems, avoid asking questions, and report numbers management wants to hear rather than numbers that are true.

*Benefit:* Problems surface early, when they are cheap to fix, rather than at the customer's site. Employees closest to the process — those with the best information about it — become willing to contribute it. Data reported to management becomes trustworthy, which is a precondition for the statistical methods the rest of the system depends on.

*Effect on net income:* Positive but indirect and difficult to quantify. There is essentially no implementation cost, since driving out fear is a change in managerial behavior rather than a purchase. The savings appear as avoided costs: fewer recalls, fewer catastrophic failures, less waste concealed until it becomes unfixable. Because the benefit is an absence of losses that never occurred, it will not appear as an identifiable line item, though it is real.

**Point 12 — Remove barriers that rob workers of pride of workmanship.**

This point includes Deming's most contested recommendation: abolition of annual performance ratings, merit pay, and management by objective. He argued that such systems rank people for variation caused by the system they work in, not by their own effort, and thereby destroy teamwork, encourage short-term numbers management, and demoralize the majority who are rated below average.

*Benefit:* Employees stop competing internally for ratings and begin cooperating on process improvement. Managers stop manipulating short-term measures to protect their own reviews. Quality improves because workers are permitted — and equipped — to do work they can take pride in.

*Effect on net income:* Positive but the least measurable of the six, and the most disruptive to implement. Eliminating appraisal machinery reduces administrative cost modestly. The larger effect operates through productivity, retention, and quality. Because it requires dismantling compensation systems that senior managers themselves rose through, this point is the most frequently ignored, and abandoning it undermines Points 5 and 8 as well.

**PART II: FIVE COMPANIES THAT IMPLEMENTED DEMING'S SYSTEM**

| Company | Adopted | Net Income, Year 1 After | Net Income, Year 2 After | Profits Increased? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ford Motor Company | 1981 | 1982: loss of $658 million | 1983: profit of $1.87 billion | Partially |
| General Motors | 1982 | 1983: $3.73 billion | 1984: approximately $4.5 billion | Yes |
| Procter & Gamble | 1986 | FY1987: $327 million | FY1988: $1.02 billion | Partially |
| Motorola | 1986 | 1987: $308 million | 1988: $445 million | Yes |
| General Electric | 1995 | 1997: $8.20 billion | 1998: $9.30 billion | Yes |

**Ford Motor Company.** Deming was engaged personally in 1981 after Ford had suffered heavy losses. In 1982, the first year following, Ford still lost $658 million — though the loss narrowed sharply from $1.06 billion in 1981. In 1983, the second year following, Ford earned a record $1.87 billion, the largest single-year earnings reversal in U.S. corporate history to that date. Ford is the strongest documented case, but it illustrates Deming's own warning that the system does not produce results in the first year.

**General Motors.** Deming consulted with GM beginning in 1982, initially through the Pontiac division. Net income rose from $963 million in 1982 to $3.73 billion in 1983 and roughly $4.5 billion in 1984 — increases in both years. GM is nonetheless a qualified case: adoption was divisional rather than company-wide, and the recovery of the domestic auto market and 1982 UAW concessions contributed materially to the results.

**Procter & Gamble.** P&G adopted Deming's quality methods in the mid-1980s. Fiscal 1987 net income fell to $327 million from $709 million, a decline driven principally by a substantial one-time restructuring charge rather than by operating deterioration. Fiscal 1988 recovered strongly to $1.02 billion, and fiscal 1989 rose again to $1.21 billion. The two-year record is therefore mixed on a reported basis and positive on an operating basis.

**Motorola.** Motorola's Six Sigma program, launched in 1986 and derived directly from the Deming statistical tradition, produced increases in both following years: net income rose from $194 million in 1986 to $308 million in 1987 and $445 million in 1988. Motorola won the first Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award in 1988.

**General Electric.** GE launched Six Sigma company-wide in September 1995. Net income rose from $6.57 billion in 1995 to $7.28 billion in 1996, $8.20 billion in 1997, and $9.30 billion in 1998 — increases in both years following implementation and every year thereafter under the program. Notably, GE's own accounting showed a *negative* net return on the program in 1996, when it spent roughly $200 million against $170 million in savings, confirming the pattern of first-year cost and later-year return described in Part I.

**Overall observation.** Four of the five companies showed higher profits within two years, and all five showed higher profits within three. However, none of these results can be attributed to Deming's system alone, since macroeconomic recovery, currency movements, and restructuring actions all contributed. Deming himself would have rejected the two-year test entirely, arguing that "the most important figures are unknown and unknowable."

---

**PART III: DEMING'S INFLUENCE ON FOREIGN COUNTRIES AND COMPANIES**

Deming's influence abroad substantially preceded his recognition in the United States and remains larger overseas than at home.

He first traveled to Japan in 1947 to assist the occupation government with census work. In 1950, the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers invited him to lecture on statistical quality control; he addressed groups of engineers and, critically, twenty-one senior industrialists who controlled a large share of Japan's industrial capital. This access to top management, rather than to quality departments alone, is what allowed his ideas to be adopted as company policy rather than as a technical specialty.

Deming donated the royalties from the published transcripts of those lectures to JUSE, which used the funds to establish the **Deming Prize in 1951**. The prize became Japan's most prestigious industrial award, and competing for it drove company-wide quality programs across Japanese industry. Winners include Toyota, Nissan, Komatsu, Bridgestone, Nippon Steel, Kawasaki Steel, Hitachi, Fuji Xerox, and Kansai Electric Power. Toyota's development of the Toyota Production System, and its later global influence on lean manufacturing, drew directly on the statistical and systems thinking Deming introduced.

In 1960, Emperor Hirohito awarded Deming the Second Order Medal of the Sacred Treasure, recognizing his contribution to Japan's postwar industrial recovery. Japanese products, widely regarded as cheap and unreliable in 1950, had become international benchmarks for reliability within two decades — a transformation that in turn created the competitive pressure forcing American manufacturers to reconsider their own methods in the 1980s.

JUSE opened the Deming Application Prize to non-Japanese companies in the 1980s, extending his influence further. **Florida Power & Light** became the first company outside Japan to win, in 1989. **Philips Taiwan** won in 1991. In India, **Sundaram-Clayton** won in 1998, followed by **TVS Motor**, **Mahindra & Mahindra**, **Rane Group**, and **Tata Steel** in 2008 — Tata Steel reporting roughly $150 million in bottom-line benefit from its quality initiatives. Thai and other Southeast Asian manufacturers have won repeatedly, making the prize a genuinely international standard.

His influence also reached Europe. **Nissan's Sunderland plant** in the United Kingdom applied Japanese quality methods on British soil and became one of Europe's most productive automobile facilities, and the Rover Group adopted comparable practices. The European Foundation for Quality Management, established in 1988, and the ISO 9000 series both incorporate principles traceable to Deming's framework — process focus, continuous improvement, and management responsibility for system performance.

---

**CONCLUSION**

Deming's system offers a coherent argument that quality is not a cost but a source of cost reduction. Of the six points examined, Points 3 and 5 produce the most direct and measurable improvement to net income; Points 4 and 6 require accepting near-term expense in exchange for later return; and Points 8 and 12 produce real but largely unmeasurable benefits by improving the accuracy of information and the willingness of employees to act on it. The corporate record supports the system's effectiveness while confirming Deming's own caution that its results appear over years, not quarters. His influence has proven durable and global, reshaping Japanese industry first and, through Japanese competitive success, American and international industry thereafter.

**SUBJECT:** Deming's 14 Points — Selected Principles, Financial Impact, and Corporate Adoption

Research into W. Edwards Deming's Total Quality Management system indicates that six of his fourteen points offer the clearest and most defensible benefits to a company considering implementation. Deming's central argument was that quality is not a cost to be balanced against profit but a source of cost reduction, and that roughly eighty-five percent of quality problems originate in the system rather than in the workers operating it. The points examined below are Point 3 (cease dependence on inspection), Point 4 (end the practice of awarding business on price tag alone), Point 5 (improve constantly and forever), Point 6 (institute training on the job), Point 8 (drive out fear), and Point 12 (remove barriers to pride of workmanship). Each is discussed in terms of the operational benefit it delivers and its likely effect on net income, followed by a review of five adopting companies and an assessment of Deming's influence abroad.

**Selected Points and Their Effect on Net Income**

**Point 3 — Cease dependence on inspection to achieve quality.** Deming held that mass inspection at the end of a production line is an admission that the process itself is out of control. Inspection does not create quality; it sorts acceptable units from defective ones after the company has already paid to produce both. The alternative is to build quality into the process through statistical control so that defects are prevented rather than detected. The benefit is the elimination of an entire category of non-value-adding work, along with reductions in scrap, rework, warranty claims, and the defects that end-of-line inspection inevitably misses. The effect on net income is positive and among the most direct of the six points. Inspection labor, rework labor, scrap material, and warranty reserves all flow through cost of goods sold, so removing them raises gross margin without any increase in sales volume. There is a timing offset, however, since measurement systems and process capability studies must be paid for before the savings materialize, and net income may therefore be flat or modestly depressed during the first year of implementation.

**Point 4 — End the practice of awarding business on price tag alone.** Deming urged companies to abandon competitive bidding based on unit price and to move instead toward long-term, single-source supplier relationships built on demonstrated quality and mutual trust. Purchasing on price alone, he argued, ignores the total cost of the transaction. A component that is two cents cheaper but arrives with inconsistent dimensions generates downstream costs far exceeding the savings, including line stoppages, rework, engineering changes, and field failures. The benefit of a stable supplier relationship is reduced incoming variation, the ability to conduct joint process improvement with the supplier, and lower administrative cost from the elimination of constant re-bidding. The effect on net income is mixed in the short term and positive over time. Unit purchase prices will very likely rise, increasing raw material cost in the first year, while the offsetting savings from lower incoming defect rates, smaller inventory buffers, and fewer production interruptions accumulate over subsequent periods. Of the six points, this is the most difficult to sell internally, because purchasing departments are typically evaluated on price variance and this point requires that measure to be abandoned.

**Point 5 — Improve constantly and forever the system of production and service.** Improvement in Deming's system is not a project with a completion date but a permanent obligation of management, institutionalized through the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle and applied to every process in the organization rather than to manufacturing alone. The benefit is that costs decline continuously as variation is removed, productivity rises without capital expenditure, and improvements compound rather than plateau. A competitor pursuing one-time fixes will eventually be overtaken by an organization improving at a constant rate. The effect on net income is strongly positive over a multi-year horizon and is likely the largest single contributor among the six points, since continuous reduction in scrap, cycle time, and rework lowers cost of goods sold while improved reliability supports both pricing power and repeat purchases. The effect in any individual quarter is small, which is precisely why Deming warned that the system cannot be evaluated on a quarterly basis and why management teams under pressure to deliver short-term earnings frequently abandon it.

**Point 6 — Institute training on the job.** Deming observed that most workers are trained by other workers who were themselves trained inadequately, so that error is transmitted through the organization rather than corrected. He called for formal, standardized training in both the job itself and the statistical methods used to control it.
